# World Design

## Purpose

The demo should feel like a place with a past, people who depend on it, and pressures that shape what happens next. Establish those relationships before adding more map content or visual detail. The map is the visible result of the world's history, resources, power, and movement.

Every named area must answer four questions: what happened here, who has a claim to it, what does it provide, and why does it matter now? A region's historical significance should explain its present-day borders, settlements, routes, customs, and conflicts. If a location has no consequential past or present role, combine it with a neighboring area or leave it unnamed.

## Area design record

Complete this record for every major area before implementing its landmarks or encounters.

### Identity and history

- **Name and common names:** What do locals call it? Are there older or rival names?
- **Historical significance:** What event, institution, person, migration, disaster, or resource made this area matter? What evidence of that history remains in the landscape?
- **Historical layers:** What changed hands or changed use over time? What does the current ruler claim happened, and what do locals remember?
- **Present consequence:** How does its history shape a current law, feud, boundary, duty, fear, or opportunity?

### Land and resources

- **Terrain and climate:** What can be crossed, farmed, defended, or settled here, and in which seasons?
- **Resources:** What food, materials, livestock, craft, or strategic assets come from the area? Who controls access?
- **Needs and exchange:** What can it not produce? Where does it trade, and along which route? What happens when that route closes?
- **Settlement pattern:** Where do people live and why? Describe the main settlement, smaller communities, abandoned sites, and working landscapes.

### People and power

- **Political arrangement:** Is the area feudal, communal, directly ruled, clerical, clan-held, chartered, occupied, or contested? Name the actual institutions and obligations rather than relying on the label alone.
- **Authority and claims:** Who rules in practice? Who holds land, collects dues, dispenses justice, commands armed force, or claims a competing right?
- **Social order:** Which groups live here, what do they owe, and what protections or privileges do they have? Who benefits and who bears the cost?
- **Local voice:** What do residents value, resent, fear, and disagree about? Avoid treating the population as a single opinion.

### Place in the wider world

- **Connections:** Which roads, rivers, passes, sea lanes, or customary paths link the area to others? Who maintains and polices them?
- **Dependencies:** What does this area need from its neighbors, and who depends on it?
- **Current pressures:** What immediate event is changing daily life? Tie threats to the area's history, ecology, resources, or political arrangements.
- **Player-facing stakes:** What can the player learn, change, protect, lose, or misunderstand here?
- **Map expression:** Which terrain, route, settlement, boundary, ruin, work site, or encounter communicates the answers above without a lore dump?

## Coherence checks

Before an area is considered designed, check that:

1. Its resources fit its terrain and climate, and its settlement pattern fits both.
2. Its political arrangement has a practical basis: control of land, labor, food, passage, law, or protection.
3. Its history has visible consequences in the present rather than existing only as a date or paragraph.
4. Its routes make economic and military sense, including who benefits from them and who can block them.
5. Its danger creates distinct consequences for residents and for the player.
6. At least one detail on the map tells the story of the place through layout, use, damage, or upkeep.

## Current anchor: Valren

Treat Valren as the first complete area design, not merely a castle backdrop. The ruling house's authority should be legible in the land around the castle: what land and obligations belong to the lord, which communities owe service or dues, who keeps roads and defenses, and what rights those communities retain. Define the historical event or bargain that established those arrangements before presenting them as settled fact.

### Esh Varen, the village beneath the castle

Esh Varen is the orchard settlement beneath Varenhold, remembered by Ushari's grandmother and named on the old household stone reused in the castle wall. Place it close enough to be part of the castle's daily life, but far enough away to have its own orchards, fields, yards, and approach road. Its fruit and preserved harvest supply the household and garrison; the castle should not appear to sustain itself in empty countryside.

Give the village a practical reason to stand at its chosen site: access to water and arable land, shelter near the castle, and a road linking it to the wider domain. Show its relationship to the ruling house through visible obligations (such as grain dues, labor, or maintaining the road) and through the protections and rights villagers receive in return. Choose the precise arrangement as Valren's history is defined; do not assume every villager is a serf or that feudal obligations are uniform.

On the map, make the village a compact cluster on the route between the castle and its fields, with a well or stream, cultivated plots, and a few work yards. The five wolf packs should threaten the village's usable space or supply routes, giving the quest a human consequence: fields left untended, interrupted deliveries, and residents sheltering behind whatever defenses they can manage. Use the pack placements to create a reason the player must travel through and around the domain, rather than surrounding the castle with unrelated encounters.

The castle, nearby settlements, fields, woodland, water, and routes should form a working domain. Establish what Valren produces, what it must import, what passage through the domain connects, and which neighboring powers or communities have a stake in that movement. The five wolf packs are a disruption to this lived system: their positions and effects should threaten specific residents, work, or connections. Clearing them should restore or change something tangible in the domain, not only reduce a map counter.

Ushari's opening question about the absent brother should also arise from this political and historical context. His journey east for the passage needs to mean something to Valren: identify what the passage is, why the brother went, who controls it, and what his absence changes for succession, security, or diplomacy. Keep these answers as design decisions before expanding the dialogue.

## Recommended design order

1. Define the area's historical significance and present consequences.
2. Define land, resources, settlement, and exchange.
3. Define political authority, obligations, rights, and conflict.
4. Connect it to neighboring areas through routes and dependencies.
5. Place map features that make this structure visible and playable.
6. Write scenes and quests that reveal or change the structure.
7. Add visual assets to reinforce the established place.

This sequence keeps narrative, mechanics, and map design grounded in the same world.
